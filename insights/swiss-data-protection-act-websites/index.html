The Swiss Data Protection Act and your website: what you actually need

Cookie banners, privacy policies, contact forms, analytics. What Swiss law really requires from a company website, and where the EU rules differ.

WEB BETTER RESEARCHPublished 22 OCTOBER, 2026

On this page

  1. The privacy notice is a legal duty
  2. Fines target people, not just companies
  3. Cookies: Swiss law is not EU law
  4. Contact forms, newsletters and analytics
  5. Records and data breaches
  6. A website checklist

Key takeaways

  • Since 1 September 2023 the revised FADP requires you to tell people, when you collect their data, at least who you are, why you process it and who receives it, including which countries it goes to.
  • Swiss law does not require consent for every cookie. You must inform visitors and let them object, and consent is needed for unexpected, high-risk or sensitive processing.
  • If you also target visitors in the EU, the GDPR can apply as well, and it requires prior consent for non-essential cookies.

This article is general information to help you plan your website. It is not legal advice. For your specific situation, talk to a lawyer who specialises in data protection.

The revised Federal Act on Data Protection, often called the nFADP or nDSG, came into force on 1 September 2023. It protects personal data of natural persons, meaning any information that relates to an identifiable person. For almost every company website that includes contact forms, newsletter sign-ups, analytics, embedded maps and videos, and the server logs that record visitors' IP addresses.

The privacy notice is a legal duty

Article 19 of the Act requires you to inform people appropriately whenever you collect their personal data. At a minimum you must give:

  • your identity and contact details as the controller;
  • the purpose of the processing;
  • the recipients or categories of recipients, if you pass the data on, for example to your hosting provider, newsletter tool or analytics service;
  • if data is disclosed abroad, the country or international body it goes to and the safeguards that apply.

In practice this information lives in a privacy policy linked from every page, with short notes where data is collected, such as next to a contact form. The duty to name countries matters more than many companies realise: most popular website tools store data in the United States or the EU, and the notice has to say so.

Fines target people, not just companies

The Act's penalties are unusual. Under Articles 60 and 61, a fine of up to CHF 250,000 can be imposed on the private persons responsible, for example for wilfully failing to provide the required information. Only intentional violations are punishable, and prosecution happens on complaint and is carried out by the cantonal authorities, as the FDPIC explains. The FDPIC itself cannot impose fines. For a website, the practical message is simple: an accurate, complete privacy notice is the cheapest protection you can have.

Cookies: Swiss law is not EU law

This is where most confusion comes from. According to the FDPIC's factsheet on cookies from March 2026, Article 45c of the Telecommunications Act allows data on a visitor's device to be processed only if users are informed about the processing and its purpose, and told that they can object. Unlike European law, Swiss law emphasises transparency and the right to object rather than requiring prior consent for every non-essential cookie.

There are important exceptions. The FDPIC states that explicit prior consent is required when processing can be considered unexpected, high-risk, or involves sensitive data, and its detailed cookie guidelines explain how to assess this. Extensive advertising tracking and profiling across sites tend to fall on the consent side.

  • Strictly necessary cookies (sessions, shopping baskets, security) need information in your privacy notice, but no banner choice.
  • Analytics and marketing cookies need clear information and an easy way to object, in principle before they are activated. For advertising tracking, plan on asking for consent.
  • Visitors from the EU: if your site is aimed at customers in the EU, Article 3 of the GDPR can apply too, and the GDPR together with the EU ePrivacy rules requires prior consent for non-essential cookies. Many Swiss companies therefore use one consent banner that satisfies both.

Contact forms, newsletters and analytics

  • Contact forms: ask only for what you need to reply (Article 7 requires data protection by default), say what happens with the message, and make sure the form is sent over HTTPS.
  • Newsletters: keep proof of sign-up and offer a simple unsubscribe link in every email. Name your newsletter provider and where it stores data.
  • Analytics: choose privacy-friendly settings, such as shortened IP addresses and no cross-site tracking, and list the tool and its location in your notice.
  • Embedded content: YouTube videos, Google Maps and social widgets send visitor data to their providers as soon as they load. Load them only after a click, or mention them in your notice.

Records and data breaches

The Act requires a record of processing activities, but under Article 12 the Federal Council sets exceptions for organisations with fewer than 250 employees whose processing poses a negligible risk. The federal SME portal explains the details. Separately, Article 24 requires you to notify the FDPIC as quickly as possible of any data security breach likely to cause a high risk to the people concerned, for example if a database of customer enquiries is exposed.

A website checklist

  1. Publish a privacy policy that names you, your purposes, your service providers and the countries data goes to, and link it in the footer of every page.
  2. List every cookie and tracking tool on the site, with its purpose and provider.
  3. Give visitors a clear way to object to non-essential cookies, and ask for consent for advertising tracking or if you target EU visitors.
  4. Reduce what forms ask for, and use HTTPS everywhere.
  5. Load third-party embeds only when needed, or disclose them.
  6. Keep an internal list of where personal data is stored, and know who handles a breach.

webbetter.ch follows these principles itself: one notice explains the Swiss FADP, the GDPR and the relevant telecommunications rules, visitors can reject everything except strictly necessary storage with one click, and they can change their choice at any time.
