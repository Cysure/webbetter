The European Accessibility Act and WCAG 2.2: what Swiss companies need to know

Accessibility is now law for many digital services in the EU, and it reaches Swiss companies that sell there. What changed, who is affected and what to fix first.

DESIGN STUDIOPublished 15 OCTOBER, 2026

On this page

  1. What the Act covers
  2. Does it apply to Swiss companies?
  3. What “accessible” means in practice
  4. Where to start

Key takeaways

  • The European Accessibility Act applies to services provided to consumers after 28 June 2025, including e-commerce, consumer banking, e-books and passenger transport services.
  • It covers companies that offer these services to EU consumers, wherever they are based. Microenterprises providing services, with fewer than 10 staff and no more than EUR 2 million turnover or balance sheet, are exempt.
  • WCAG 2.2, published in October 2023, adds nine success criteria, including minimum target sizes and focus that is never hidden.

This article is general information, not legal advice. Whether the Act applies to your business depends on your services and markets; check with a specialist.

For years, web accessibility was mostly a matter of good practice for private companies. The European Accessibility Act, Directive (EU) 2019/882, changed that. EU member states had to apply its rules from 28 June 2025, and they now cover a range of everyday digital products and services sold to consumers.

What the Act covers

The European Commission lists the products and services in scope. For websites and apps, the important ones are:

  • E-commerce, meaning any online shop or booking service selling to consumers;
  • Consumer banking services;
  • E-books and the software used to read them;
  • Passenger transport services by air, bus, rail and water, including websites, apps and ticketing;
  • Electronic communications and access to audiovisual media services.

Products such as computers, smartphones, ticket machines and e-readers are covered too. A purely informational company website, a portfolio or a B2B site that does not sell to consumers is generally not in scope, although accessibility is still good practice there.

Does it apply to Swiss companies?

Switzerland is not in the EU, but the Act is about where you sell, not where you are based. The directive applies to covered products placed on the EU market and covered services provided to consumers in the EU, and it does not exempt companies headquartered outside the Union. A Swiss online shop delivering to Germany, Austria or France is therefore affected.

There is one important exemption. The directive defines a microenterprise as one with fewer than 10 employees and an annual turnover or balance sheet total of no more than EUR 2 million, and microenterprises providing services are exempt from the service requirements. There are also transitional periods: for example, existing service contracts can run on unchanged until they expire, for at most five years.

What “accessible” means in practice

The Act sets functional requirements rather than a checklist, but in practice compliance is measured against the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines. The current version, WCAG 2.2, became a W3C Recommendation on 5 October 2023. It keeps everything from WCAG 2.1, removes the obsolete Parsing criterion and adds nine new success criteria. The ones at levels A and AA, which is the level organisations usually target, are:

  • Focus Not Obscured (Minimum), AA: when someone tabs to a link or button, it must not be completely hidden behind sticky headers, chat widgets or cookie banners.
  • Dragging Movements, AA: anything that needs dragging, such as a slider or a map, must also work with a single click or tap.
  • Target Size (Minimum), AA: clickable targets must be at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels, or have enough space around them.
  • Consistent Help, A: help options such as contact details or chat must appear in the same place on every page.
  • Redundant Entry, A: do not make people type the same information twice in one process, for example a delivery and billing address.
  • Accessible Authentication (Minimum), AA: logging in must not depend on a memory or puzzle test without an alternative. Allowing password managers and copy-paste usually covers it.

Where to start

  1. Check whether you are in scope: do you sell covered services to consumers in the EU, and are you above the microenterprise threshold?
  2. Test the key journeys: product search, basket, checkout, account creation and login, with a keyboard only and with a screen reader.
  3. Fix the common failures first: missing image descriptions, low colour contrast, form fields without labels, and buttons that are not real buttons.
  4. Publish an accessibility statement describing how your service meets the requirements and how users can report problems.
  5. Build it into the process: accessible design systems and automated checks in development cost far less than retrofits.

Accessibility is often treated as a compliance cost. In our experience it is closer to a quality standard: clear contrast, generous tap targets, proper labels and pages that work without a mouse make a site faster and easier for everyone, and they are the same signals search engines and AI assistants rely on to understand a page.
