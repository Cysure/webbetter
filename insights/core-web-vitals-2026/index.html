Core Web Vitals in 2026: speed is still a ranking story

The quiet compounding of a fast site: better rankings, cheaper ads, higher conversion. A practical checklist from our rebuilds.

ENGINEERINGPublished 28 SEPTEMBER, 2026

On this page

  1. The three numbers
  2. What Google actually says about ranking
  3. What speed is worth
  4. Measure the right thing
  5. The checklist we use on every rebuild

Key takeaways

  • Good means LCP within 2.5 seconds, INP of 200 milliseconds or less and CLS of 0.1 or less, measured at the 75th percentile of real visits.
  • Google says good Core Web Vitals align with what its ranking systems reward. Relevance still comes first, so speed is a tiebreaker you should not lose.
  • The business case is measured: in Vodafone's A/B test a 31% faster LCP brought 8% more sales.

Every few years someone declares that page speed no longer matters for SEO. Every few years the data says otherwise. Speed was never the strongest ranking signal, and it still is not. But it touches everything that comes after the ranking: whether the visitor stays, whether the form gets filled in, whether the ad click you paid for turns into a customer.

The three numbers

Google measures page experience with three Core Web Vitals. Each has a clear threshold for "good".

2.5 sLargest Contentful Paint: when the main content appears
200 msInteraction to Next Paint: how fast the page reacts
0.1Cumulative Layout Shift: how much the layout jumps

Two details are easy to miss. First, the thresholds apply at the 75th percentile of page loads, separately for mobile and desktop. A fast test on your office laptop proves little. Three in four real visits, on real phones and real networks, have to be good. Second, INP replaced First Input Delay as a Core Web Vital in March 2024. FID only measured the delay before the first interaction. INP looks at the responsiveness of interactions across the whole visit, which is much harder to fake and much closer to how a slow site feels.

What Google actually says about ranking

Google's Search documentation says good Core Web Vitals align with what its core ranking systems seek to reward, and recommends achieving them for success in Search. It does not promise that fast pages outrank relevant ones, and nobody should claim it does. A slow page with the best answer will usually beat a fast page with a weak one.

In practice speed acts as a tiebreaker between pages that are similarly relevant. In competitive markets that is exactly where you are: several agencies, shops or clinics offering much the same, with comparable content. Losing the tiebreaker is avoidable.

What speed is worth

The stronger argument for speed is commercial, and here the evidence is better than the folklore.

  • Vodafone ran an A/B test on a landing page, identical except for performance work. The optimised version had a 31% better LCP and produced 8% more sales, a 15% better lead-to-visit rate and an 11% better cart-to-visit rate.
  • Deloitte, in a study commissioned by Google, found that a 0.1-second improvement in mobile speed across the whole journey lifted retail conversions by 8.4% and travel conversions by 10.1%. Retail shoppers also spent 9.2% more per order.

Those gains compound. A faster page converts more of the traffic you already have, which lowers your cost per lead from ads, which lets you buy more traffic at the same budget. Speed is one of the few improvements that pays off in SEO, paid media and conversion at the same time.

Measure the right thing

Lab tools such as Lighthouse are useful for debugging, but they simulate one visit on one device. Google assesses your pages with field data from real Chrome users, collected in the Chrome UX Report and shown in the Core Web Vitals report in Search Console. Optimise until the field data turns green. A perfect Lighthouse score with poor field data is still a poor site.

The checklist we use on every rebuild

  1. LCP: serve the hero image in a modern format at the right size, never lazy-load it, and give it fetch priority. Preload the main web font, or use a system font.
  2. LCP: render the main content as HTML from the server or the edge. Waiting for a JavaScript bundle before showing the headline costs seconds on mid-range phones.
  3. INP: ship less JavaScript. Remove unused libraries, split code by route, and defer third-party scripts until they are needed.
  4. INP: keep event handlers short. Break long tasks up so the browser can paint between them, and show feedback immediately.
  5. CLS: reserve space for every image, video, embed and ad with width and height or aspect-ratio. Never insert banners above content after load.
  6. Everything: audit tag managers and chat widgets every quarter. On most sites we review, third-party scripts are the single biggest cost.

We practise what we preach: webbetter.ch shows its own live Largest Contentful Paint, layout shift and responsiveness in the footer of every page, measured in your browser as you read.

Speed rarely makes headlines in a strategy deck. It is not as visible as a new brand or as exciting as AI search. But it quietly decides how much of everything else you do actually pays off.
