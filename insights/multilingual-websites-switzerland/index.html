Multilingual websites in Switzerland: getting German, French, Italian and English right

Four national languages, plus English for business. How to structure a Swiss website so every visitor, and Google, finds the right version.

ENGINEERINGPublished 19 OCTOBER, 2026

On this page

  1. One URL per language
  2. Hreflang: telling Google which page is which
  3. Let visitors choose
  4. Translation is not localisation
  5. Quick checklist

Key takeaways

  • Give every language its own URL, such as /de/ and /fr/. Google advises against switching languages with cookies, browser settings or IP addresses.
  • Connect the versions with hreflang. Each page must list itself and every other version, and the links must be reciprocal or Google may ignore them.
  • Translate whole pages, not just menus, and localise for the market. Google determines a page's language from its visible content.

Switzerland is one of the few markets where a serious company website almost always needs more than one language. In the Federal Statistical Office's 2019 figures, 62.7% of the population named German as a main language, 22.8% French and 8.3% Italian, with 6.5% naming English (people could name several). A Zurich company that only speaks German online is invisible to a fifth of its home market.

Getting the languages right is partly a translation question and partly a technical one. The technical part decides whether Google shows a French speaker in Lausanne your French page or your German one.

One URL per language

Google's guidance on multilingual sites recommends a separate URL for each language version. Content that changes language based on a cookie, the browser's language setting or the visitor's IP address is the most common mistake we see on Swiss sites, and Google warns it may then not find and crawl all your variations. Its crawler mostly visits from the United States and does not send a preferred language, so it may only ever see one version.

For most Swiss companies the simplest structure is subdirectories on one domain: example.ch/de/, example.ch/fr/, example.ch/it/ and example.ch/en/. Google lists low maintenance as the advantage. Separate country domains make sense when you operate as separate businesses in different countries, not when you serve one country in four languages.

Hreflang: telling Google which page is which

Hreflang annotations tell Google that two pages are the same content in different languages. Google's documentation sets a few strict rules:

  • Reciprocity. If the German page points to the French page, the French page must point back. Without return links, Google may ignore the annotations.
  • Self-reference. Each language version must list itself as well as all the others.
  • Valid codes. Use a language code (de, fr, it, en) with an optional region (de-CH, fr-CH). A region on its own is invalid.
  • A fallback. Add x-default for visitors whose language you do not serve, usually your English or main-language page.
  • Absolute URLs, including https://, in the HTML head, HTTP headers or the XML sitemap. One method is enough.

A detail specific to Switzerland: if you only have one German version, you can mark it as both de and de-CH. Swiss users get the Swiss page, and German speakers in Germany and Austria still find it.

Let visitors choose

Google advises against automatically redirecting visitors to another language. A German-speaking manager in Geneva, or an English-speaking expat in Zug, should not be forced into a version they did not ask for. Suggest a language based on the browser if you like, but always show a visible language switcher that links to the same page in the other language, not back to the homepage.

Translation is not localisation

Google determines a page's language from its visible content, and it warns that translating only the navigation while the content stays in another language creates a poor experience. Beyond that, good multilingual sites adapt rather than just translate:

  • Swiss conventions. Swiss Standard German uses ss instead of ß, prices are written as CHF 1’250.–, and phone numbers start with +41.
  • Search terms differ by language. People in Romandie do not search for a French translation of your German keyword. Research each language separately.
  • Titles and descriptions. Each version needs its own title and meta description, written for that language, not copied.
  • Machine translation is a draft. It is a fine starting point, but have a native speaker review anything customers will read.

Quick checklist

  1. One URL per language, in subdirectories on your .ch domain.
  2. Hreflang on every page: itself, every other version and x-default, reciprocal and absolute.
  3. The lang attribute and visible content in the same language on every page.
  4. A language switcher on every page that links to the equivalent page.
  5. No automatic redirects based on IP address or browser language.
  6. Separate keyword research, titles and descriptions per language.

webbetter.ch is built this way in English and German: real pages for each language at their own address, reciprocal hreflang including de-CH, a switcher that keeps you on the same page, and Swiss spelling throughout.
