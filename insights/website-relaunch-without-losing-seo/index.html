SEO, RELAUNCH · 4 MIN READ

How to relaunch your website without losing your Google rankings

A new website should bring more customers, not fewer. Most traffic drops after a relaunch are avoidable. Here is the process we follow, step by step.

ENGINEERINGPublished 26 OCTOBER, 2026

On this page

  1. Why relaunches lose traffic
  2. Before launch: map every URL
  3. Redirects done right
  4. Launch day checklist
  5. The first eight weeks

Key takeaways

  • Map every old URL that has traffic or links to its closest new equivalent before launch, and redirect it with a permanent 301 or 308.
  • Google advises keeping redirects for at least a year and going straight to the final URL, without chains.
  • Expect some movement in rankings for a few weeks. A sharp, lasting drop usually means broken redirects, blocked pages or lost content.

A relaunch is one of the riskiest moments in a website's life. Years of rankings are tied to specific addresses, and a redesign tends to change them all at once. We have seen companies lose half their organic traffic overnight because a new site went live without a single redirect. We have also seen relaunches that grew traffic from the first week. The difference is almost never the design. It is the preparation.

Why relaunches lose traffic

Google ranks individual pages, not websites. When a page's address changes and nothing tells Google where it went, the old URL returns an error, its rankings fade, and links from other sites point to nothing. The same happens when content that ranked well is cut or merged during the redesign, or when the new site is accidentally blocked from crawling.

Google's own guide to site moves is clear that some movement is normal: it says to expect temporary fluctuation in rankings and that a small to medium-sized site can take a few weeks for most pages to move. Normal fluctuation is a dip that recovers. A lasting drop is a sign that something was missed.

Before launch: map every URL

The single most important document in a relaunch is a URL map, a simple table with every old address on the left and its new home on the right. Google recommends building exactly this, because the same mapping drives your redirects, your internal links and your sitemap.

  1. Collect the old URLs. Export them from a crawl of the live site, your XML sitemap, Google Search Console (Pages and Performance reports) and your analytics. Include PDFs and image URLs that get traffic.
  2. Prioritise. Mark the pages that bring organic visits, conversions or backlinks. These need a perfect match.
  3. Match like for like. Each old page should point to the new page that answers the same question. Sending everything to the homepage is treated by Google much like a missing page.
  4. Keep what ranks. If an old page ranks well, carry its content, headings and title over, and improve them rather than starting from scratch.

Redirects done right

Google's redirect documentation distinguishes between permanent and temporary redirects. A permanent redirect (301 or 308) tells Google the new address should become the indexed one. A temporary redirect (302 or 307) tells it to keep showing the old address. For a relaunch, you want permanent redirects, set up on the server.

  • Redirect straight to the final page. Google advises against chains. If a page has moved twice over the years, point the oldest URL directly at the newest one.
  • Keep them for a long time. Google recommends keeping redirects for as long as possible, generally at least one year. We keep them indefinitely, because old links keep sending visitors for years.
  • Avoid JavaScript redirects. Google says to use them only when server-side or instant meta refresh redirects are impossible, because rendering can fail.

Launch day checklist

  1. Remove any noindex tags and robots.txt blocks that protected the staging site. This is the most common, and most expensive, mistake we see.
  2. Test a sample of old URLs from your map, especially the high-value ones, and confirm each returns a single 301 to the right page.
  3. Make sure each new page has a self-referencing canonical tag and that internal links point to new URLs, not to redirects.
  4. Submit the new XML sitemap in Google Search Console. If the domain itself changed, also use Search Console's Change of Address tool, which Google says is only needed for moves between domains or subdomains.
  5. Check Core Web Vitals and mobile rendering on the live site, not just on staging.

The first eight weeks

Watch Search Console daily for the first two weeks, then weekly. The Pages report shows whether new URLs are being indexed and old ones are being dropped as redirected. The Performance report shows clicks and impressions per page, so you can compare the new pages with the old ones they replaced.

A useful rule of thumb: if total organic clicks are still more than 20% below pre-launch levels after six weeks, stop waiting and investigate. Look for 404 errors on old URLs, pages that are crawled but not indexed, and content that ranked before but no longer exists.

Done carefully, a relaunch is a chance to grow: faster pages, clearer structure and better content usually rank better than what they replaced. The redirects simply make sure you keep everything you have already earned while you build on it.
