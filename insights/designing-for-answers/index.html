DESIGN, STRATEGY · 3 MIN READ

Designing for answers, not just clicks

When the first touchpoint is an AI summary, your brand has to survive being paraphrased. Here is how we design for it.

DESIGN STUDIOPublished 09 SEPTEMBER, 2026

On this page

  1. What the data shows
  2. Two audiences, one page
  3. How we design for it
  4. What does not change

Key takeaways

  • Pew Research found people clicked a result in 8% of Google visits with an AI summary, against 15% without one.
  • The visit that does arrive is later in the journey and more deliberate. The page has to confirm the answer and close the deal quickly.
  • Design now has two audiences: the person, and the model summarising you to that person. Clear facts, a distinctive voice and visible proof serve both.

For most of the web's history, design started from an assumption so basic nobody wrote it down: the visitor arrives at your homepage, or at least on one of your pages, and forms an impression there. That assumption is weakening. More and more often the first thing a potential customer learns about you is a sentence written by someone else: an AI model summarising your site, alongside two competitors, in answer to a question.

What the data shows

The clearest numbers come from Pew Research Center, which analysed the actual browsing of 900 US adults in March 2025. When a Google results page included an AI summary, people clicked a traditional result in 8% of visits. Without a summary they did so in 15% of visits. Links inside the summary itself were clicked in just 1% of visits, and people were more likely to end their browsing session altogether: 26% of the time against 16%.

Ahrefs, using Search Console data from 300,000 keywords, found that an AI Overview correlated with a 34.5% lower click-through rate for the top-ranking page in March 2025. Its follow-up with December 2025 data put the drop at 58%. These are correlations rather than experiments, but they all point the same way.

This does not mean websites matter less. It means the website is no longer where the first impression happens. It is where the decision gets confirmed.

Two audiences, one page

A page now has to work for a person scanning it in seconds, and for a model reading it to summarise. The good news is that they want largely the same things. The bad news is that much of what passes for modern web design serves neither: vague hero slogans, key facts hidden in carousels, case studies without numbers, and pages that read the same as every competitor's.

How we design for it

  • Say what you do in the first line. "We design and build fast websites for Swiss SMEs" survives a summary. "Elevating digital experiences" does not. If a model cannot tell from your hero what you sell and to whom, neither can a busy buyer.
  • Turn claims into facts. Replace "experienced team" with how many projects, since when, for whom. Specific numbers get quoted, and the research on generative engines shows statistics and quotations measurably raise how much of a page makes it into answers.
  • Give every page one clear job. A page that answers one question well is easier to cite than a page that half-answers five. Use headings that read as questions, and put the answer directly underneath.
  • Keep the brand recognisable in paraphrase. Your name, your positioning line and your proof points should be worded identically everywhere: site, profiles, directories, press. Consistency is how a summary ends up describing you the way you describe yourself.
  • Make proof visible and checkable. Logos of real clients, linked case studies, named testimonials and published prices are trust signals for people and evidence for machines. Put them in HTML text, not only in images.
  • Design the landing for someone who already knows the answer. Visitors from an AI answer arrive with context. Lead them to proof, pricing and contact fast, rather than restarting the story from the beginning.

What does not change

A distinctive brand matters more in this world, not less. When three agencies are described in the same neutral sentence, the one people remember is the one whose name, look and voice stuck the last time they saw it. Summaries flatten copy. They cannot flatten a memorable experience once someone clicks through.

Design for the click you still get, and for the sentence written about you when you do not.

Web Better design principle

That is the brief we now give ourselves on every project: a site that a model can summarise accurately, and that a person who arrives is glad they clicked.
